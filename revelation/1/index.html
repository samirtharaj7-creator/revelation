Study Notes

Revelation 1:1

Revelation opens by naming itself an unveiling of Jesus Christ. The book is not introduced as a sealed riddle or a playground for religious speculation, but as a disclosure God intended His servants to receive. The movement of the message is orderly and heavenly: God gives it to Christ, Christ sends it by angelic mediation, John receives it, and the servants of God are meant to hear it. That chain of communication gives the book both authority and mercy.

The word translated "Revelation" carries the idea of uncovering what was hidden. The book therefore begins with grace: God is willing to make known what His people need in order to be faithful. The phrase "signified it" also matters. Revelation communicates through signs, symbols, scenes, and echoes of earlier Scripture. Its symbolic language is not permission for imagination to run wild; it is a call to read carefully with Daniel, the prophets, the sanctuary, and the rest of Scripture open beside it.

The phrase "things which must shortly come to pass" should be handled with care. It does not require every event in the book to be exhausted in the first century, and it does not allow the prophecy to be postponed entirely to the end. A historic reading of Daniel and Revelation sees the prophecy beginning in John's own world and unfolding through the experience of the church toward the final triumph of Christ. Nearness is covenantal and prophetic: the decisive work of Christ has begun, and the church now lives inside the era of fulfillment.

For the reader, the first verse establishes the right attitude. Revelation is from Jesus and about Jesus. Its purpose is not to make believers anxious, but to make them watchful, obedient, and hopeful. The servants of God are not left blind in history. They are given enough light to follow the Lamb with intelligence and trust. The book begins, not with beasts or plagues, but with the generosity of God who speaks.
