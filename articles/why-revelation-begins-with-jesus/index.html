Reading Revelation

Why Revelation Begins with Jesus

Revelation begins as the unveiling of Jesus Christ, so every symbol, warning, judgment, and promise must be read from the Lamb outward.

7 min readJesus ChristLambprophecyworshiphope

Not a closed book

The opening words of Revelation are already a safeguard against confusion. The book does not introduce itself as the hiding of Jesus Christ but as His unveiling. It is given by God, mediated through Christ, communicated by an angel, written by John, and entrusted to the servants of God. That chain matters because it tells the reader that Revelation is not a private code for the clever. It is a prophetic gift for the church.

This means the book should not be approached first through fear, speculation, or curiosity about catastrophe. The first subject is Jesus. He is the faithful witness, the first begotten of the dead, the ruler of the kings of the earth, the Son of man among the lampstands, the slain Lamb before the throne, the rider who judges in righteousness, and the light of the New Jerusalem. The beasts, plagues, Babylon, and final conflict are real, but they are never the center.

Christ before conflict

Revelation places Christ before the church before it shows the church facing the world. In chapters 2 and 3, Jesus walks among the lampstands and speaks to His people with both tenderness and searching authority. He knows their works, their suffering, their compromise, their poverty, their weariness, and their hidden faithfulness. Before the reader meets the dragon in chapter 12 or the beasts in chapter 13, the reader has already seen the risen Christ holding His church in His hand.

That order is not accidental. Prophetic interpretation becomes distorted when it treats history as though evil is the main actor. Revelation allows the conflict to become intense, but it never allows Satan to become ultimate. The dragon can rage, the beast can coerce, Babylon can intoxicate, and the nations can tremble, yet the throne remains occupied and the Lamb remains worthy.

Revelation 1:12-20Revelation 2:1Revelation 12:17Revelation 13:8

The Lamb's way of victory

The heart of Revelation's theology is not that Christ wins by becoming a larger version of the beast. He conquers as the Lamb that was slain. John hears of the Lion of the tribe of Judah, but when he looks he sees a Lamb bearing the marks of sacrifice. The victory of Christ is therefore cruciform before it is cosmic. He is worthy to open the scroll because He has conquered through His death, resurrection, and heavenly authority.

This gives Revelation its moral shape. The saints overcome by the blood of the Lamb and by faithful witness. They follow the Lamb wherever He goes. They keep the commandments of God and the faith of Jesus. The book is not merely announcing that Christ will defeat His enemies; it is showing how Christ forms a people whose loyalty mirrors His own.

Revelation 5:5-9Revelation 12:11Revelation 14:4Revelation 14:12

Hope with open eyes

Revelation is honest about suffering. It does not pretend the church will move through history untouched by persecution, deception, spiritual famine, or compromise. Yet it refuses despair because the suffering of God's people is seen from the sanctuary, the throne, and the promise of restoration. The prayers of the saints rise before God. The martyrs are not forgotten. The sealed servants are not abandoned. The New Jerusalem is not wishful thinking but the destination of the Lamb's work.

To begin with Jesus is therefore not to soften Revelation. It is to read it with the right gravity. The judgments are serious because the Lamb is holy. The warnings are urgent because the Lamb is merciful. The final invitation is generous because the Lamb has already paid the cost of redemption.

Revelation 6:9-11Revelation 8:3-5Revelation 21:1-5Revelation 22:17

Why This Matters

If Jesus is the center, Revelation becomes a book of worship, endurance, and hope rather than a maze of frightening images. The reader is free to study beasts, seals, trumpets, Babylon, and final judgment carefully, because none of them displaces the Lamb.

The first discipline of reading Revelation is therefore simple: keep asking what each vision reveals about Christ, His ministry, His people, and His final purpose for the world.
